81.5M Set to Travel as FAA Seeks $50B and ICE Mask Law Is Blocked

AAA expects 81.5 million Thanksgiving travelers, the Trump administration asks Congress for $50.1 billion to fix air traffic control, a judge blocks parts of Connecticut's ICE law, and the Senate permitting bill faces a tough road.

A busy week across the country

Thanksgiving is days away, and millions of Americans are getting ready to travel at a time when flying and driving both cost more. This week also brought a big funding request to fix the nation's aging air traffic control system, a federal court ruling on how far a state can go in regulating immigration agents, and fresh steps on a major energy and infrastructure bill in the Senate. Here is a plain-English guide to the biggest US stories right now.

AAA Expects 81.5 Million Thanksgiving Travelers

AAA forecasts that about 81.5 million Americans will travel at least 50 miles from home between Tuesday, November 24, and Monday, November 30. That is a 0.1% dip from last year's 81.6 million and ends a three-year run of record Thanksgiving travel. Around 72.9 million people are expected to drive, down slightly from 73.1 million, and nearly 90% of travelers will go by car. About 5.96 million are expected to fly domestically, a 0.2% decrease, and round-trip domestic tickets average $800, up 17% from last year. Buses, trains and cruises are the one area of growth, up 4.1% to about 2.6 million people. Stacey Barber, AAA's vice president of travel, said costs are a factor, partly because of higher travel costs this year, but that travelers won't notice much of a difference on roads and at airports because volumes remain close to record levels.

The Trump Administration Asks for $50.1 Billion for Air Traffic Control

On Tuesday, November 17, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy, FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford and White House budget director Russ Vought asked Congress for $50.1 billion to modernize the air traffic control system and airport infrastructure. The request followed a nationwide ground stop the previous Friday that lasted about four hours. The Department of Transportation said outdated equipment disrupted aircraft communications and radar feeds, forcing the FAA to halt flights across the country. The money would be split into five $10 billion pieces covering a new digital system for controllers, airport infrastructure, terminal upgrades, five new regional control centers to replace 22 aging ones, and up to 140 control towers. The newest of the current centers dates back to 1969. In their letter, the officials said Congress should provide vital funding for the modern, resilient aviation system the American people deserve. Whether lawmakers agree is still to be decided.

Judge Blocks Parts of Connecticut's Law on ICE Agents

On Friday, November 20, US District Judge Vernon Oliver issued a preliminary injunction blocking four sections of Connecticut's S.B. 397, a 2026 law meant to regulate US Immigration and Customs Enforcement agents. Three sections would have allowed the state's Inspector General to examine and prosecute federal agents, and one would have made it a misdemeanor for officers to wear masks while dealing with the public. Oliver found the provisions likely violate the Supremacy Clause, writing that the law plainly imposes conditions on how federal officers must conduct and present themselves. The lawsuit, filed by the Justice Department in May, continues. Governor Ned Lamont's office said it was disappointed by today's ruling, and Attorney General William Tong's office said it is reviewing the decision and evaluating next steps. Republican lawmakers said they had warned the law was unconstitutional.

The Senate's Permitting Bill Moves Forward, but Not Yet Over the Line

In Washington, a bipartisan bill to speed up approvals for energy, transmission and infrastructure projects cleared a key Senate test vote, 73-25, on Monday, November 9. Republicans voted 51-1, while 21 Democrats and independent Angus King voted yes. It is not law yet. Senators will file amendments, the full Senate must still pass the bill, and the House has to act on whatever comes out of it, with the lame-duck calendar leaving little time. Minority Leader Chuck Schumer has said he has outstanding concerns, and Democratic Senator Catherine Cortez Masto opposes final passage unless historic preservation changes are dropped. Sheldon Whitehouse, a co-author, said, This bill is the floor, not the ceiling, of our climate and environmental aspirations. Wind power is a flashpoint, because President Trump has said he would not approve wind turbines.

What to Watch Next

Keep an eye on three things. First, Thanksgiving week travel, including weather and airport delays, as the holiday rush begins on Tuesday. Second, how Congress responds to the air traffic control request and whether it becomes part of this year's spending bills. Third, whether Connecticut appeals the ruling on its ICE law, and whether the Senate takes up amendments to the permitting bill before the year ends. Check each story for updates before you publish.
