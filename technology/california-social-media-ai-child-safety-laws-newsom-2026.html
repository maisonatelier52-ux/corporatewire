California Passes Sweeping Social Media and AI Rules to Protect Children Online

California approved child-safety legislation covering social-media design, AI chatbots and minors’ privacy. The package creates new duties for platforms, with implementation and legal challenges still to follow.

Matthew Collins | Technology & Corporate Affairs Contributor • September 11, 2026
SHARE
Explainer

What to know

California approved child-safety legislation covering social-media design, AI chatbots and minors’ privacy. The package creates new duties for platforms, with implementation and legal challenges still to follow.

California Governor Gavin Newsom has signed a sweeping package of legislation aimed at protecting children from the potential dangers of social media platforms, artificial intelligence tools, and online services. The new rules are among the most sweeping steps taken by states in the U.S. to regulate how technology companies interact with young users.

The package includes 13 individual measures to deal with concerns about addictive digital features, online privacy, AI chatbot safety, and harmful content involving children. The bill aims to reduce risks associated with technologies that lawmakers say can threaten children’s mental health, safety, and personal data.

One of the most important steps is to stop social media platforms providing addictive features like infinite scrolling and autoplay content to under-16s. The law seeks to restrict design features that promote extended use among young people.

The law also gives more protection for AI chatbot systems. Chatbot program developers will face new safety assessment, parental control, and risk management procedure requirements when their products interact with minors.

“The goal of this legislation is to make sure technology is supporting kids’ health and well-being, not preying on their vulnerabilities,” Newsom said. Supporters of the measures say companies should be more responsible for the effect of their products on young users. The California move comes amid a national debate over how social media affects children. The proposals are like ones lawmakers have introduced in several other states as concerns over online addiction, cyberbullying, privacy risks, and access to harmful material have grown.

They also point to the special status of California as the home of some of the largest technology companies, including Meta, Google, and other digital platforms. The state is a central focus in the debate over how governments should regulate powerful online services, focusing on how to balance innovation and free expression.

New Accountability Requirements for Digital Channels and Chat Bots

California’s new rules come amid rising concerns over artificial intelligence systems and their interactions with children. Lawmakers have also said that AI companions and chatbot tools should have more protection because young users may form emotional attachments to such systems or use them for sensitive conversations.

One measure, called “Adam’s Law,” adds new requirements to operators of AI chatbots, including safety procedures, parental controls, and independent assessments of potential risks. The law is named for Adam Raine, a teen whose family said he died after talking to an AI chatbot.

The legislation also imposes criminal penalties on child sexual abuse material that is added to material involving minors that has been digitally altered or generated by artificial intelligence. The change is intended to help combat the growing misuse of artificial intelligence to generate harmful images, officials said.

Another measure would ban the manufacture and sale of toys with AI companion chatbots for four years. Supporters of the measure said products for children that use conversational AI need more oversight because of concerns about privacy, data collection, and emotional influence.

The new rules will force AI developers to think more about the potential harms of their products before they are released to the public. Companies may need to carry out more extensive testing and establish stricter safeguards to meet the requirements.

Lawmakers are calling on tech companies to make their digital products safer and more kid friendly. Some platforms have been faulted for prioritizing engagement and growth over protections for younger users.

California law is part of a broader pattern of how governments are grappling with artificial intelligence. Regulators are looking at not only the usual online safety issues but also the ways modern technologies might affect children's development, privacy, and mental health.

The rules could influence future AI regulation beyond California as other states and governments consider ways to manage fast-developing technologies.

New Rules for Social Media Firms to Protect Children

The new California laws impose new obligations on social media companies, especially in designing platforms for younger users. Rather than simply attacking what is posted online, the state is going after features lawmakers believe lead to overuse.

The new restrictions will require platforms to restrict engagement-promoting features for under-16s. “The goal is to reduce addictive patterns of use that could lead to mental health problems for teens,” state legislators say.

Advocates of the bill say social media platforms have a lot of power over user behavior through recommendation algorithms, notifications, and personalized feeds. They say platforms have a responsibility to create safer spaces for minors.

These include enhanced privacy protections and requirements for companies to consider potential risks associated with their products. Regulators want companies to identify potential harms before they get to children.

Some tech groups have criticized the new rules, saying they could raise privacy concerns, limit access to online communities, or place too many burdens on companies. The Electronic Frontier Foundation and other digital rights groups have expressed concerns about effects on free speech and access to the Internet.

Rules could also affect how services are personalized and delivered, warned by the owners of big social platforms. Online platforms already spend a lot on safety systems, they say, and government rules should not have unintended consequences.

Supporters say there is a need for stronger regulation, while critics say voluntary measures by technology companies have not gone far enough. They say children need more protection as they are more vulnerable to online dangers.

California’s move is part of a broader national push to regulate social media companies. Other states, like New York and Utah, have adopted similar policies to address concerns over youth's online safety.

California’s Digital Safety Laws May Shape the Nation’s Tech Policy

California’s new child safety laws could have national repercussions, changing the way tech companies build products across the country. Many big tech firms have a footprint across the country, meaning state rules can influence how the industry does business.

The legislation comes as technology companies face increased scrutiny. Governments, researchers, and families have raised concerns about the link between digital platforms and issues such as mental health, privacy, and online safety.

Supporters say the rules California imposes could become a blueprint for future regulation. They say stronger standards are needed as artificial intelligence becomes more integrated into everyday products and services.

The laws also reflect how lawmakers are moving from dealing with traditional internet problems to regulating modern technologies. There is an increasing examination of the possible effect of AI chatbots, digital assistants, and social media platforms on vulnerable groups.

But the effectiveness is likely to depend on how the measures are implemented. Companies in the tech space will need to adapt systems, create compliance procedures, and even reimagine some functions to meet the new requirements.

The battle over what California is doing reveals a broader dilemma for governments worldwide: how to protect users, while still letting innovation thrive. Regulators will have to strike a balance between safety concerns and issues of privacy, competition, and freedom of expression.

The state’s move could be a harbinger of a future in which tech companies embed safety assessments into product development. Much like with cybersecurity and privacy reviews, companies may face increasing pressure to consider the impact of their digital products on younger users.

As social media and AI advance, it is likely that governments will add additional rules to protect children on the internet. California’s law is one of the most ambitious attempts so far to put limits on technology companies that operate in the digital era.
