Black Friday Online Sales Hit $9.7B as AI Shopping Agents Arrive

US shoppers spent $9.7 billion online by Friday evening, up 12.8%, as AI shopping agents debut, Google sets a February deadline for Play developers and Nvidia tightens China rules.

Technology Takes Center Stage on Black Friday

This Black Friday, technology is as much a part of the story as the discounts. American shoppers pushed online spending to new highs, AI agents that can shop on your behalf are being tested at checkout, and two big tech companies announced rule changes that will matter to app makers and chip buyers. If you are trying to follow the latest US technology news, here is a plain-English summary of what happened, why it matters and what to watch next.

Online Shoppers Spend $9.7 Billion by Friday Evening

According to Adobe Analytics data reported by Reuters, US shoppers spent $9.7 billion online by 6:30 p.m. Eastern on Black Friday, up 12.8% from a year earlier. Adobe expected the full day to land between $13 billion and $13.4 billion, ahead of its earlier September forecast of $12.9 billion. Thanksgiving Day itself set a record of $7.1 billion in online spending, with phones accounting for 63.2% of it. Adobe's final Black Friday total had not been published at the time of writing, so the full-day number may differ from the forecast.

Cyber Monday and the Five-Day Shopping Weekend

Adobe is forecasting about $6.1 billion in online spending on Saturday and $6.4 billion on Sunday, followed by $15.1 billion on Cyber Monday, which is up 6.2% from last year. For the full five-day stretch from Thanksgiving through Cyber Monday, it projects $47.5 billion, up 7.4%. Salesforce, which measures sales differently, is predicting about $18.7 billion in US online sales on Black Friday, up 4%. The two companies use different methods, so their numbers should not be compared directly.

AI Agents Head to Checkout

A newer part of the story is AI shopping agents, which can search, compare and even buy for you. IBM reported on November 25 that OpenAI recently launched ChatGPT Wallet, which lets its agents make purchases but is so far limited to US Pro subscribers, and that Meta's Muse agent has been shopping for users since September. Safeguards include a human approval step with Face ID or a fingerprint and a one-time card number. Still, trust is limited: a Deloitte survey found only 16% of US shoppers would let AI finish a purchase. IBM's Alexis Zamkow said, My agent will compete with your agent, and added that brands that win will treat their product data as the storefront.

Retailers Are Still Setting the Rules for Agents

Not every store is rolling out the welcome mat. IBM's report says Amazon blocked Muse in September because the agent did not identify itself, though the companies were reportedly in talks to lift the block as of November 25. United Airlines lets AI agents look up fares but not buy tickets, while Walmart gives Muse its own account profile with a spending limit that the shopper sets. For everyday buyers, the takeaway is simple: check what an agent is allowed to do on your behalf and keep an eye on the approval screen before any purchase goes through.

Google Sets a February Deadline for Android Developers

App makers have a date to mark. Google says that from March 1, 2027, Google Play store listings will show legal details about developers who sell to the public, including contact information and, for personal accounts, a phone number and address. Every existing developer account must complete a trader status declaration by February 1, 2027. Developers who miss it risk being unable to push app updates, and their apps could be removed worldwide. The change is tied to UK and European consumer-protection rules, and developers who do not distribute there can declare non-trader status. For solo developers who work from home, it is worth reading Google's help pages before the deadline.

Nvidia Tells Cloud Partners to Cut Exposure to China

In chips, the Financial Times reported that Nvidia has told cloud and data center partners they must sell at least 51% of their services to non-Chinese customers, a rule that applies to future contracts rather than existing leases and was conveyed verbally rather than in writing. An unnamed Singapore-based cloud group told the FT that the rule will definitely hurt business if strictly applied. A Nvidia spokesperson told Bloomberg that outdated US export controls are restricting US firms. These reports have not been confirmed in writing by Nvidia, so details could change.

What to Watch Next in Tech

Three things are worth watching. First, Adobe's final Black Friday number and its Cyber Monday result on November 30, which will show whether AI-driven shopping traffic keeps growing. Second, whether Amazon and Meta settle their dispute over shopping agents. Third, any written guidance from Nvidia on its China rule, along with Google's reminders ahead of the February 1 developer deadline. As always, be careful with deals that look too good to be true, use one-time or virtual card numbers where you can and treat any message asking for your payment details with suspicion.
