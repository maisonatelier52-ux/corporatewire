China’s Huawei Technologies Faces Racketeering Trial in New York

Federal prosecutors in Brooklyn prepare to try Chinese telecommunications giant Huawei on sweeping criminal RICO, trade secret theft, and sanctions evasion charges in a defining legal showdown between Washington and Beijing.

Danielle Brooks | Senior Policy & US Markets Analyst • Sept. 08, 2026
SHARE

In what legal scholars and tech executives describe as one of the most consequential corporate criminal prosecutions in United States history, federal prosecutors in New York are preparing to bring Chinese telecommunications powerhouse Huawei Technologies Co., Ltd. before a jury on charges of racketeering conspiracy, systematic trade secret theft, and sanctions evasion.

The high-stakes trial, set to take place in the United States District Court for the Eastern District of New York (EDNY) at Cadman Plaza in Brooklyn before U.S. District Judge Ann Donnelly, represents the culmination of an extraordinary multi-year criminal investigation spanning several presidential administrations. The Justice Department alleges that Huawei functioned for decades not merely as an aggressive commercial enterprise, but as an illegal racketeering syndicate designed to illicitly acquire American technology and circumvent Western financial sanctions.

“

This trial marks the intersection of federal criminal jurisprudence, cutting-edge telecommunications supremacy, and great-power geopolitical rivalry between Washington and Beijing.

The Superseding Indictment: Unpacking the RICO Allegations

At the heart of the government's case is a sweeping 16-count superseding indictment built under the Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act (RICO)—a statutory framework historically deployed against mafia syndicates and violent cartels, but increasingly utilized by the Department of Justice to prosecute complex, institutional corporate enterprises.

Federal prosecutors assert that beginning in the early 2000s, Shenzhen-based Huawei and its affiliates orchestrated a systematic corporate campaign to misappropriate proprietary intellectual property from at least six major American tech companies. According to the indictment, targeted technologies included source code for high-capacity internet routers, cellular antenna radio frequency architecture, dynamic wireless networking patents, and proprietary robotic smartphone testing equipment developed by domestic carriers such as T-Mobile.

Prosecutors further allege that Huawei maintained a confidential internal policy of awarding monetary bonuses and corporate promotions to employees who successfully obtained non-public intellectual property from competitors. When confronted with civil disputes and federal inquiries, the government contends that company operatives engaged in obstruction of justice by destroying internal communications, staging false internal audits, and misrepresenting their operational ties to corporate proxies.

Sanctions Evasion, Skycom, and Covert Operations in Iran

Beyond intellectual property theft, the New York indictment charges Huawei with orchestrating an extensive conspiracy to defraud major international banking institutions regarding its business operations in Iran, Syria, and North Korea, directly violating United States economic sanctions and export control regimes.

Central to these counts is Huawei's relationship with Skycom Tech Co. Ltd., an Iranian telecommunications entity that prosecutors characterize as a wholly controlled corporate shadow subsidiary. The government alleges that Huawei executives misled international financial institutions—including HSBC and Standard Chartered—into clearing hundreds of millions of dollars in transactions tied to banned hardware deliveries to Iranian telecom carriers, falsely claiming that Skycom was an independent business partner.

The long-running case previously triggered international diplomatic friction in December 2018 when Huawei Chief Financial Officer Meng Wanzhou, daughter of founder Ren Zhengfei, was detained at Vancouver International Airport on a U.S. extradition warrant. While Meng resolved her individual charges under a 2021 deferred prosecution agreement that allowed her return to China, the federal corporate indictment against Huawei Technologies itself was maintained and expanded.

Huawei's Legal Defense: Commercial Dispute or Geopolitical Weaponization?

Huawei has vigorously maintained its innocence across all counts, deploying a high-profile legal defense team led by veteran American litigators. In pretrial motions submitted to Judge Donnelly, defense counsel argued that the Justice Department is improperly transforming conventional civil patent disputes and contractual disagreements into criminal racketeering charges.

"The government's theory would convert standard commercial negotiations and decades-old patent disputes—many of which were resolved long ago through private civil settlements—into an existential criminal enterprise," defense attorneys wrote in court filings. Huawei contends that the indictment represents a politically motivated effort by Washington to handicap a global technological competitor and establish legal justification for sweeping trade embargoes.

The defense is also challenging the extraterritorial reach of U.S. sanctions laws, arguing that foreign transactions denominated in global clearing currencies should not expose non-American corporate entities to federal racketeering liability without clear statutory congressional authorization.

Geopolitical Stakes: Semiconductor Sanctions and Global Tech Decoupling

The upcoming trial in Brooklyn comes at a time of heightened technological confrontation between the United States and the People's Republic of China. Over the past decade, Washington has barred Huawei from federal 5G infrastructure, pressured European allies to remove its hardware from national networks, and implemented stringent Department of Commerce export controls restricting Huawei's access to advanced American semiconductors, electronic design automation (EDA) software, and foundry services.

Despite severe trade restrictions, Huawei has mounted a resilient resurgence in domestic markets, unveiling self-developed 7-nanometer and 5-nanometer mobile processors, proprietary Ascend AI compute clusters, and the HarmonyOS NEXT independent operating system. For Beijing, the trial is viewed as an ideological and economic flashpoint, with Chinese foreign ministry spokespersons regularly condemning U.S. legal actions against domestic national champions as "unilateral technological hegemony."

Potential Verdict and Industry Implications

If convicted on the RICO and fraud charges, Huawei faces catastrophic financial remedies, including billions of dollars in criminal fines and mandatory forfeiture of illicitly obtained revenues. More critically, a criminal conviction in federal court would legally institutionalize Huawei's ban across Western commerce, empower transatlantic regulators to mandate total hardware rip-and-replace orders, and trigger secondary sanctions against third-party enterprises doing business with the Chinese telecom titan.

As jury selection approaches at the Cadman Plaza federal courthouse in Brooklyn, legal experts, semiconductor executives, and national security officials across Washington and Shenzhen are preparing for a trial that will redefine the boundaries of corporate espionage, international sanctions enforcement, and global technology sovereignty for the coming decade.
