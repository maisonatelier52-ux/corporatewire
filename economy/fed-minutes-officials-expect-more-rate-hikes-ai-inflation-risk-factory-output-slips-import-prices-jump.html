Fed Minutes Show Almost All Officials Expect More Rate Hikes as Factory Output Slips

Fed minutes show almost all officials expect more rate hikes, with AI spending and energy costs adding to inflation worries, as factory output slips and import prices jump.

Christopher Reed | Business and Economy Contributor • November 19, 2026

The Fed tells Americans more rate hikes are probably coming

If you were hoping for lower borrowing costs anytime soon, this week's economic news is not encouraging. Minutes from the Federal Reserve's October meeting, released on Wednesday, show that almost all policymakers believe more interest rate increases are likely to be needed to bring inflation back to 2%. Alongside that, new data shows factory output slipped in October, while the prices of goods coming into the country jumped. The labor market, meanwhile, has remained steady. Here's a plain-English look at what the Fed's minutes say, why artificial intelligence has become part of the inflation conversation and what the latest economic data means for your wallet.

What the Fed Minutes Say About More Rate Hikes

The Fed left its key interest rate unchanged at a range of 3.75% to 4% at its October 28 meeting, in a 10-2 vote. Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack dissented, preferring a quarter-point increase. The minutes show that almost all officials judged that further rate rises would likely be appropriate, in line with the Fed's September projections, which pointed to one more hike this year. Some of those who favored holding said it was a close call, and some who wanted a hike said they could have accepted waiting because markets already expect higher rates. Officials also warned that delaying could require a larger and more disruptive tightening later. The next meeting is on December 8 and 9, and traders see another quarter-point rise as very likely.

Could December Bring a Bigger Rate Hike?

The minutes also reveal that the debate is not entirely settled. Two unnamed officials said the Fed should consider a rate rise larger than a quarter point in December if inflation data disappoints, according to Breitbart's report on the minutes. Fed Governor Christopher Waller made a similar case when he spoke in London on November 10, saying a half-point hike \"should be considered at the December meeting\" if core inflation keeps rising by about 0.3% or more. \"Hiking only 25 basis points would put our credibility at risk,\" he said. The Fed's preferred inflation gauge, the PCE price index, rose 3.7% over the past year in the latest data, with core PCE at 3.1%. Not everyone agrees. New York Fed President John Williams has reportedly said he saw no need to consider a move of that size, so the final decision is still open.

Why the Fed Is Worried About AI and Energy Prices

One of the more unusual details in the minutes is how often artificial intelligence comes up. Many officials said the AI build-out is a significant source of inflation risk, because the investment surge could push demand above supply if capacity expands more slowly than expected. Strained supplies of data-center equipment are spilling over into consumer electronics and other industries. Most officials said inflation pressures were broad-based, and the vast majority said high energy prices continue to add to inflation. Several officials thought that the effect of tariffs on prices appeared to be fading, which is one of the few pieces of good news in the minutes. For households, that means the cost of fuel, electronics and everyday goods may stay elevated for a while longer, even if the pace of increases slows.

Factory Output Slips While Import Prices Jump

The week's data was a mixed picture. The Federal Reserve reported on Tuesday that US industrial production fell 0.4% in October, compared with a 0.1% gain that economists expected, after a 0.7% rise in September. A large part of the drop came from utilities, which fell 4.3%, while mining dropped 0.8%. Manufacturing, however, rose 0.3%, helped by a 4.4% jump in motor vehicles and parts, and excluding those it edged down 0.1%. Capacity utilization eased to 76.4%. On the price side, reports show import prices rose 1.1% in October, above the 0.7% economists forecast, with export prices also up 1.1% against an expected 0.5%. Higher import costs can feed into the prices shoppers pay, which is the sort of pressure the Fed is worried about. The Fed also plans an annual revision to its industrial production figures on November 24.

Shoppers and Workers Are Holding Up

Despite the inflation worries, the underlying economy looks steady. Retail sales rose 0.2% in October to $748.2 billion, in line with forecasts, and sales excluding autos and gasoline were up 0.6%, better than the 0.4% economists expected. The labor market also looks stable. Initial jobless claims were 200,000 in the week ending November 7, and the four-week average was about 195,250, while continuing claims were around 1.7 million. Claims have stayed in a range of about 189,000 to 212,000 since mid-July, a low level by historical standards. The minutes describe the job market as stable and growth as strong, although officials noted higher borrowing costs are weighing on housing, commercial real estate and small businesses. A strong economy is good news for workers, but it can make it harder for the Fed to ease up on inflation.

What to Watch Next in the US Economy

There are several things to keep an eye on. First, this morning's jobless claims report, which covers the week ending November 14 and which analysts expect to show claims near 200,000. A sudden jump would be an early sign of layoffs. Second, the Fed's revised industrial production figures on November 24, which could change the story of how much factories have slowed. Third, any further comments from Fed officials ahead of the December 8 and 9 meeting, especially on whether they favor a quarter-point or half-point rise. For everyday households, the message is that borrowing costs are likely to stay high for some time. If you have variable-rate debt, it may be worth paying it down or locking in a fixed rate where you can.
