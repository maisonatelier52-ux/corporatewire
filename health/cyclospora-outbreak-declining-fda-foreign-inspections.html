Cyclospora Outbreak Followed Years of Declining FDA Foreign Inspections

Federal health surveillance data links a multi-state surge in foodborne Cyclospora parasitic infections to a sharp decline in on-site overseas farm inspections by the Food and Drug Administration, exposing structural gaps in the imported produce safety net.

Amanda Whitaker | Senior Financial & Healthcare Correspondent • Sept. 08, 2026
SHARE

A nationwide surge in cases of cyclosporiasis—a severe gastrointestinal disease caused by the microscopic parasite Cyclospora cayetanensis—has renewed intense scrutiny on federal food safety enforcement, following federal audit disclosures that the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) drastically curtailed on-site inspection visits to foreign agricultural operations over recent years.

According to epidemiological data compiled by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), hundreds of laboratory-confirmed illnesses across more than thirty states have been linked to contaminated fresh produce items commonly imported from Central and South America, including fresh raspberries, blackberries, cilantro, and packaged salad blends. Epidemiologists report that the timing of these seasonal spikes directly mirrors an unprecedented retreat in physical overseas oversight by federal regulatory inspectors.

When on-the-ground foreign farm inspections drop by over 60%, the American produce aisle becomes an unmonitored frontline for waterborne tropical parasites.

The Anatomy of a Regulatory Retraction: GAO and Audit Findings

An exhaustive review of inspection registries maintained by the FDA’s Human Foods Program and independent analyses by the Government Accountability Office (GAO) reveals that federal physical farm inspections in major exporting nations fell to historically low levels. While the Food Safety Modernization Act (FSMA) of 2011 originally envisioned a robust, expanding overseas inspectorate, budget limitations, investigator retention deficits, and administrative travel barriers caused foreign produce audits to crater by more than 60 percent compared to pre-2020 baselines.

In critical growing regions throughout Mexico, Guatemala, and Peru—where billions of dollars in fresh fruits and culinary herbs are cultivated annually for American supermarket distribution—the FDA inspected only a tiny fraction of registered agricultural growing operations. Regulators increasingly substituted in-person soil and water testing with desktop document audits under the Foreign Supplier Verification Programs (FSVP), creating what food policy analysts describe as a dangerous regulatory vacuum.

"The statutory premise of FSMA was prevention rather than reaction," explained Dr. Elena Rostova, a senior food safety epidemiologist and former Department of Agriculture advisor. "However, paper-based compliance checks cannot detect whether an irrigation canal in a foreign farming valley was inundated with human sewage runoff three weeks prior to harvest. Physical oversight is irreplaceable."

Why Cyclospora Eludes Conventional Washing and Disinfection

Unlike bacterial contaminants such as Salmonella or Escherichia coli (E. coli), which are often vulnerable to chemical sanitizers like peracetic acid and chlorinated wash flumes, Cyclospora cayetanensis oocysts are encased in a remarkably durable, chemically impervious double-layered protein wall.

The parasite is human-specific, meaning it cannot replicate in animals or produce tissue, but enters agricultural fields exclusively through human fecal contamination. When agricultural laborers lack adequate sanitary facilities or when untreated wastewater is used for crop irrigation, foliar pesticide spraying, or post-harvest washing, the oocysts adhere firmly to the delicate, textured surfaces of raspberries, herbs, and leafy greens.

Once consumed by an unsuspecting shopper or restaurant diner, the oocysts excyst in the small intestine, penetrating epithelial cells and triggering severe, recurring watery diarrhea, intense abdominal cramping, nausea, low-grade fever, and debilitating weight loss that can persist for up to eight weeks if left untreated with specialized sulfa-based antibiotic regimens.

The Import Reality: Over 60% of Fresh Fruit Enters via Border Ports

The inspection shortfall highlights an inescapable reality of the modern American diet: the United States now imports more than 60 percent of its fresh fruit supply and nearly 40 percent of its fresh vegetables, driven by consumer demand for year-round availability of non-seasonal produce.

Customs and Border Protection (CBP) and FDA import inspection stations at major entry ports—such as Nogales, Arizona, and Pharr, Texas—physically inspect and microbiologically test less than 2 percent of arriving refrigerated produce shipments. With perishable cargo moving rapidly through interstate distribution networks to prevent spoilage, contaminated lots frequently reach retail grocery shelves and restaurant prep kitchens before federal surveillance laboratories complete genetic sequencing of pathogen samples.

Congressional Demands and Proposed Food Safety Overhauls

In response to mounting patient caseloads and watchdog disclosures, bipartisan leaders on the House Committee on Energy and Commerce and the Senate HELP Committee have launched inquiries into the FDA’s foreign inspection strategy. Lawmakers are pressing FDA Commissioner Robert Califf for detailed timelines on restoring pre-pandemic foreign inspection quotas, expanding rapid DNA-based field testing, and establishing mandatory automated water testing protocols for all foreign certified exporters.

Advocacy coalitions, including the Center for Science in the Public Interest (CSPI) and Stop Foodborne Illness, are simultaneously calling for legislative reforms that would condition import licenses on verifiable, third-party genomic testing of agricultural water sources in exporting countries, shifting the financial burden of safety verification directly onto international corporate agribusinesses.

As public health agencies work to trace the remaining sources of the ongoing seasonal outbreaks, infectious disease clinicians urge primary care physicians to maintain high diagnostic suspicion for cyclosporiasis among patients presenting with prolonged gastrointestinal distress, noting that routine stool cultures do not detect the parasite without specific ova-and-parasite microscopy or molecular PCR panels.
